Twelve Years and Holding: Why St. Andrews Owners Stay, and What Finally Moves Them

America’s homeowners now stay a record 12 years before selling, and St. Andrews closings have fallen from 78 in 2021 to 33 last year. Here is why owners stay, what finally moves them, the tax locks on a sale, and whether more homes are coming.
By Edmund Bogen · November 10, 2026 · Written with the assistance of artificial intelligence — every figure sourced below

Last week the National Association of Realtors released its 2026 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers, and one number jumped off the page for me. The typical seller had owned their home for 12 years before selling. NAR calls it an all-time high. A year earlier it was 11 years, and in the early 2000s it was about six. Americans are staying put longer than at any point the survey has measured, and nowhere do I feel that more than inside our own gates.

The drought inside our gates

I pulled every closed sale at St. Andrews Country Club from the MLS, year by year. In 2021 we had 78 closings. Then 42 in 2022, 37 in 2023, 44 in 2024 and just 33 in 2025. Through November 10 of this year we have 31. Right now there are 12 homes for sale and 5 under contract.

Put that against the size of the community. LINKS Magazine puts St. Andrews at about 725 homes. In 2021 roughly one home in nine changed hands. Last year it was closer to one in 22. That is not a market with weak demand. It is a market where the people who own here simply are not leaving.

Why people actually move

When owners do sell, it is almost always because life changed, not because the market did. In NAR's 2025 survey, the most common reasons were wanting to be closer to friends and family (26 percent), a home that had become too small (10 percent) or too large (10 percent), a change in family situation such as marriage, a birth or a divorce (8 percent), retirement (7 percent), a job move (6 percent), and upkeep that had become too difficult (5 percent). In this year's survey, being closer to friends and family again led the list, at 22 percent.

There is one more reason that rarely makes the survey, and in a community like ours it matters a great deal: the end of a chapter. A home passes to a surviving spouse, or to children who live elsewhere. According to Cotality data reported by National Mortgage News, inherited homes made up a record 7 percent of all U.S. property transfers in 2025, and some of those homes never reach the open market at all. They stay in the family.

"St. Andrews doesn't have a demand problem. It has a staying problem. The people who own here love it, and the tax code pays them to stay."

Three locks on the front door

The capital gains lock. Federal law lets a homeowner exclude $250,000 of gain on the sale of a primary residence, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if they owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years (IRS Publication 523). Those limits have not been raised since 1997, according to NAR. Here is an illustration, not anyone's real numbers: a couple who paid $1.2 million and sells for $3.5 million has a $2.3 million gain. After the $500,000 exclusion, $1.8 million could be taxable. At the top federal long-term rate of 20 percent plus the 3.8 percent net investment income tax, both confirmed for 2026 by Kiplinger, that is up to roughly $428,000 before improvements and selling costs are counted. Meanwhile, if the owner holds the home until death, the heirs' tax basis generally steps up to market value under Section 1014 of the tax code, and that gain is never taxed. That one rule keeps a lot of homes off the market.

The property tax lock. Florida's Save Our Homes cap holds a homestead's assessed value to the lesser of 3 percent or inflation each year (Florida Statutes 193.155). After 12 or 20 years, a long-time owner's assessed value can sit far below what the home would sell for. Sell, buy something else in Florida, and the new home starts at market value. Portability softens that: you can carry the difference to your next Florida homestead, up to $500,000, as long as you establish the new homestead by January 1 of the third year after leaving the old one and apply by March 1 (Palm Beach County Property Appraiser). The catch is the cap. A home worth $3.5 million with a $2 million assessed value has a $1.5 million gap, and only $500,000 of it travels.

The rate lock. Nationally, 78 percent of outstanding mortgages carry rates below 6 percent (RISMedia, citing fourth-quarter 2025 data). For many St. Andrews owners this is the smallest of the three locks. The Census Bureau reports that about 39 percent of U.S. homeowners have no mortgage at all, and I would expect that share to be higher here, though I have no local figure to prove it.

If you want to move, you have more options than you think

Selling and paying the full tax bill is not the only path. These are the conversations I have most often with owners, and every one of them belongs in front of your CPA or estate attorney before you act.

  • Portability. Moving within Florida, including within Palm Beach County, lets you carry up to $500,000 of your Save Our Homes savings. If you buy something smaller, a proportional share travels.
  • Timing around a spouse's death. A surviving spouse who has not remarried can still use the $500,000 exclusion if the home is sold within two years of the death (IRS Publication 523).
  • An installment sale. Spreading payments over years spreads the gain over years too (IRS Topic 705).
  • A charitable remainder trust. An irrevocable trust can sell an asset, pay you an income stream and leave the remainder to charity, deferring tax on the sale (IRS).
  • Holding for heirs. The step-up at death is real. Gifting the home during life does the opposite: the recipient takes your original basis (26 U.S.C. 1015).

Two things that do not work the way people hope. A 1031 exchange does not apply to a primary residence; the IRS says property used primarily for personal use does not qualify. And renting out all or substantially all of a homestead can be treated as abandoning it, which costs you the exemption and the Save Our Homes cap (Florida Statutes 196.061).

So will St. Andrews get more inventory?

Not suddenly. Freddie Mac estimates there will be about 9.2 million fewer baby boomer homeowner households by 2035, and its researchers describe that release as "more like a tide" than a wave. Realtor.com estimates about 13.9 million homes owned by boomers and older owners will change hands within a decade, as reported by Newsweek. But a share of those will pass to heirs rather than to the market, and AARP finds three in four adults over 50 want to stay in their current homes.

Amendment 3 adds weight to the same side of the scale. Starting in 2027, a homesteaded owner's non-school exemption rises to $150,000, then to $250,000 in 2028 (Seminole County Property Appraiser), while Save Our Homes and portability stay as they are. That rewards staying. The one thing that could really loosen supply is a change to the capital gains exclusion. The More Homes on the Market Act would double it to $500,000 and $1 million and index it to inflation, but as of August 2026 it had not passed (Real Estate News).

My read, and it is opinion: expect a slow, steady trickle at St. Andrews, not a flood. Homes will come to market one chapter at a time, often with little notice, and the best of them will sell quickly. For buyers, that means getting your financing and your wish list ready now, and telling me what you are looking for so I can call you before a home is public. For owners, it means scarcity is on your side, and the right plan for your taxes matters as much as the right price.

What this means for you

If you are thinking about a move in the next few years, the time to plan is well before the listing. Run your capital gains and portability numbers with your advisers. Know what your home would bring today. Then decide on your own timeline, not the market's. I'm happy to walk through the market side with you, quietly and with no obligation.
