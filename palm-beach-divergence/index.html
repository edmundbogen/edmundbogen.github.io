The Country Says Buyer's Market. Palm Beach County Says Otherwise.

What the July numbers actually show — and the one place the national headline lands here.

I'm writing this from two perspectives at once.

The first is the one you're reading everywhere else: buyers have regained the wheel. Redfin's July data gave that headline fresh legs — a record-low count of active homebuyers against a near-record surplus of sellers.

The second is what I see on my own screen. I live in St. Andrews Country Club, I list and sell from Miami Beach to Palm Beach, and my data says something close to the opposite.

Both are real. Understanding why is worth more to you than either headline alone.

What Actually Happened

MIAMI REALTORS® released July data on August 17 and 18. It is still the current read.

Palm Beach County single-family sales rose 12.7% year over year — 1,185 closings in July 2025 to 1,336 in July 2026. That is the strongest performance among South Florida's three largest counties:

The county ended July with roughly 4,590 single-family homes for sale, down more than 23% from a year earlier. Single-family dollar volume rose 43.2% to approximately $1.7 billion. Sales at $1 million and above rose 36.5%, from 326 to 445 — and million-dollar-plus properties represented roughly 30% of the county's single-family transactions, the highest share among the three counties in MIAMI's report.

Single-family homes went to contract in a median 41 days, down from 46. Sellers closed at 95% of list price. Cash accounted for 41.2% of single-family closings and 47.7% of all Palm Beach County closings — against roughly 26% nationally.

At 3.7 months of single-family supply, that is not a buyer's market. By any conventional definition, that is a seller-favored market with a firming price floor.

The 65-Mile Divergence

The sharpest illustration of the split is not Palm Beach County versus America. It's Palm Beach County versus Miami.

Redfin estimates active buyers against active sellers in every major metro. By that measure, Miami is the most buyer-favorable major metro in the country — 154% more sellers than buyers in July, worse than June's reading.

West Palm Beach was one of only five metros in that same index where the seller surplus shrank month over month. Over the four weeks ending August 23, Redfin recorded West Palm Beach's median sale price up 10.2% year over year — the largest gain of any major U.S. metro — with pending sales up 4.7%. For context, the national median rose 2.0% (NAR), and the S&P Cotality Case-Shiller national index rose 1.5% in June — a gain that, set against 3.5% inflation, means U.S. home values have now fallen in real terms for 13 consecutive months. Palm Beach's price growth is real. The nation's is not.

"Anyone selling a house in Boca who is being counseled off a national statistic is being counseled off the wrong county."

Sixty-five miles of I-95 separate the metro with the largest seller surplus in Redfin's index from one of the few where that surplus is shrinking. That is the divergence.

The Narrative vs. The Reality

Here's where it gets interesting — and where most coverage gets it wrong.

The "national buyer's market" claim does not come from months of supply. It can't. NAR's July existing-home sales report, released August 11, showed sales down 1.7% from June but up 0.7% from a year ago, with 4.6 months of national supply and the median price up 2.0%. The traditional balance line is six months. On that yardstick, the national existing-home market is not a buyer's market at all — it is slow and broadly balanced. Stalled, not glutted.

The buyer's-market claim comes from that Redfin buyers-versus-sellers count. In July, Redfin counted 966,752 buyers — a record low in at least 13 years of tracking — against 1,462,921 sellers, a 51.3% seller surplus, and classified nearly 80% of the major metros it tracks as buyer's markets. Redfin senior economist Asad Khan put it plainly: "Buyers are dropping out faster than sellers, giving the buyers who remain more options and more negotiating power."

In plain English: nationally, inventory is not high. Demand is thin. Those are different problems, and they call for different strategies. So when a client forwards you a national headline, the honest answer is not "that's wrong." It is: that measure is real, and it is measuring something that isn't happening here.

And note what that includes. Boca's absorption fell to 5.2 months with condos in the number. Which brings us to the part of the national story that is actually correct.

Where the Headline Is Right: Attached Product

I want to be precise here, because the sloppy version of this point is worse than not making it.

The condo-supply overhang is the defining feature of the Florida market right now — but it is not evenly distributed, and it is not primarily a Palm Beach County story. Here is July, existing condos and townhouses:

Palm Beach County is the only tri-county condo market with rising prices — its fifth consecutive month of gains — and at 6.7 months it sits inside the range MIAMI classifies as balanced, not distressed. Condo sales rose 18.6% while active condo inventory fell 19.3%. That said, condo sellers hold less leverage than single-family sellers here: roughly 92% of original list, 69 days to contract.

Two more cautions. First, these are resale numbers — MIAMI's totals largely exclude new construction, preconstruction and condo conversions, so resale data alone can't tell you whether the new-development pipeline is over- or under-supplied. That is a separate market requiring separate data. Second, the real dividing line in attached product is not the county line — it is building age and building finances.

The Date Nobody Is Discussing

This is the part I'd put in front of any client who owns, or is considering, a unit in an older building.

Florida's post-Surfside framework is now largely in force. HB 913, signed June 23, 2025, moved the Structural Integrity Reserve Study completion deadline to December 31, 2025, raised the reserve-item threshold from $10,000 to $25,000, and permitted a two-year pause in reserve contributions following a milestone inspection. It also explicitly allows associations to fund repairs through special assessments, lines of credit, or loans on a majority owner vote.

One date is still ahead: associations whose milestone inspection is due on or before December 31, 2026 may complete the SIRS concurrently with that inspection. After the initial study, a SIRS must be updated at least every ten years per building. This is an ongoing obligation, not a one-time filing — which is exactly why the financial picture inside a building can change between the day you tour it and the day you close.

Florida OPPAGA published the first hard accounting of what these inspections have turned up (Report 26-04, July 2026):

  • 8,736 completed phase-one milestone inspections reported across 2024–2025, against roughly 11,700 required
  • 1,575 completed phase-two inspections against roughly 2,170 required — a gap of about 600
  • 54 buildings declared unsafe or uninhabitable across eight counties; of the 24 declared in 2025, vacancy status was reported for only five — and none of those five had been vacated
  • 1,587 deadline extensions granted, roughly 94% in coastal areas
  • Average repair-permit value rose from $337,229 in 2024 to $496,236 in 2025 — a 47% jump in one year, with individual applications ranging up to $30 million

One more finding worth sitting with: reporting compliance is poor, and Palm Beach County was among the worst — 44% of municipal officials did not report 2025 data. The absence of a red flag on a building here is not the same as a clean bill of health.

Key FactIn an older building, the meaningful number is not the list price. It is the reserve study, the milestone inspection status, and the assessment schedule. I read those before I discuss price.

Where I Could Be Wrong

I don't want to hand you a one-sided argument, because the counter-case is not weak.

July closings are backward-looking. Those 1,336 single-family sales went under contract in May and June, when Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed sat near 6.46–6.49%. It printed 6.66% on August 27, just off the 6.69% peak set August 6 — the highest rates of 2026, all clustered in this summer. As NAR's Lawrence Yun noted, "the highest mortgage rates of the year hit right in the middle of summer."

Forward indicators softened. National pending home sales fell 2.3% month over month in July to their lowest level since January; in the South, contract signings were down 2.2% on the month and 3.0% on the year.

And a 12.7% gain is measured against a base. July 2025 was a slow month. Some of this strength is arithmetic.

None of that reverses the inventory picture — 28% fewer active listings in Boca is not a base-effect artifact. But it does mean the correct posture is this market is tight right now, not this market is immune. If September and October show pendings rolling over locally, I will say so in this newsletter.

What This Means, By Situation

If you're selling a single-family home between Miami Beach and Palm Beach

You are operating in a roughly 4-to-5-month market against a national narrative that says you should be nervous. Price to the local absorption rate, not to a national headline. Countywide, single-family sellers are closing at 95% of list on 41 days to contract — a 4-to-5% negotiation, not a capitulation. Pricing 10% under comparable sales to "meet the market" concedes far more than this market is actually asking for.

If you're buying

Redfin's buyer-to-seller measure puts your leverage in Miami-Dade, in attached product, or in buildings whose reserve math has already scared other buyers off. A genuine buyer's market exists in South Florida — just not where you're standing.

If you own a unit in a building over 30 years old

Get the milestone inspection status and the current SIRS in hand before you do anything else. The repair-cost trend line is moving the wrong direction, fast, and reserve obligations are ongoing rather than one-and-done.

If you're relocating from the Northeast

Cash is doing real work here — 47.7% of all Palm Beach County closings. If you're financing at 6.66% against buyers who aren't, you need a sharper offer structure, not a bigger number.

From My Perspective at St. Andrews

The thing I keep noticing on the ground is how little the national conversation resembles the conversation inside the club communities.

I hear about buildings, boards, assessments and reserve studies constantly. I almost never hear anyone in a single-family club home worry that they can't sell. The listings that come to market in these communities are not sitting; the inventory that has genuinely thinned is exactly the product most of my neighbors own.

That is what a two-speed market looks like from inside it. Not a boom and not a bust — a market where the answer to "how is it out there?" depends entirely on which door you're standing in.

The Bottom Line

The national tape and the Palm Beach tape are both accurate. They are measuring different things in different places.

Nationally, supply is still short of balanced and buyers are missing — a slow, stalled market, not a glut. In Palm Beach County, inventory is thin and buyers showed up: a 12.7% gain in single-family sales, a 28% drop in Boca's active listings, and absorption tightening every month since March in both Boca Raton and Jupiter.

The one place the national story lands here is attached product in aging buildings, and even that is a Miami-Dade and Broward problem far more than a Palm Beach one.

Bring me a national headline and a Palm Beach address and I will show you the gap between them, in numbers, in about ten minutes. That gap is the whole opportunity.
