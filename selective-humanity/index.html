Selective Humanity

Why I stopped trusting the world's outrage meter — and what the research actually shows.
By Edmund Bogen · August 12, 2026 · Written with the assistance of artificial intelligence — all claims independently fact-checked against the sources below

Let me get my biases out of the way first, because you deserve to know them before you read another word.

I am Jewish. I am a Zionist. I feel strongly — sometimes painfully strongly — about what has happened to Israel in the court of world opinion since October 7, 2023. If you think that disqualifies me from writing this, you should stop reading now, and I won't hold it against you.

But here is what I'd ask in return. I didn't write this from feeling. I commissioned two independent research reports, from two different AI systems, and told both of them to go where the evidence went. I asked for the strongest case against my own position. Some of what came back was uncomfortable. I've included it.

Because the question I want to ask isn't "is Israel innocent?" That's not a question research can answer, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

The question is narrower and, I think, harder to dodge: when the world decides which human suffering to see, what is it actually using to decide?

Let's break it down.

The numbers nobody puts side by side

Gaza is a genuine catastrophe. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and I'd ask you not to let anyone tell you I did. UNICEF reports roughly 700,000 children in Gaza have missed formal in-person schooling for nearly three academic years, with more than 97% of school buildings damaged. Peer-reviewed mortality work in The Lancet estimates tens of thousands of violent deaths. That is real, and it deserves serious study.

Now put it next to what else is happening on the same planet, at the same moment.

These figures measure different things and shouldn't be stacked into a league table of suffering. Sudan's 12 million displaced and Gaza's 700,000 children out of school are not the same unit of misery, and I won't pretend they are.

"But you cannot look at that table and conclude that the world's attention is tracking the world's need."

Here is the part that should stop everyone cold, whatever they think of Israel: in 2025, the UN General Assembly adopted fifteen resolutions concerning Israel and roughly a dozen concerning every other country on earth combined. Zero on Sudan. That is not a talking point. That's the voting record.

The countries that get a pass

Iran. Beginning December 28, 2025, protests over a currency collapse became a nationwide uprising, and the regime answered with live fire. On January 8, the government cut the internet and the killing escalated.

I want to be precise here, because the numbers are contested and I'd rather be credible than dramatic. The Iranian government admits 3,117 dead. Human rights monitors documented at least 6,126 by late January — a figure they have since revised above 7,000. Iran International, working from leaked documents, put the total near 36,500. Time reported a hospital list showing 30,304 protest-related deaths registered on January 8 and 9 alone. Human Rights Watch called the killings unprecedented in the country's history. Amnesty International called January 2026 the deadliest period of repression in decades of its research.

So: somewhere between six thousand and thirty-six thousand people, most of them in a single catastrophic week, shot in the head and torso by their own government — and the true figure is unknowable because the regime turned off the lights first.

Where were the encampments? Where were the walkouts? Where were the union resolutions? Where was the semester of teach-ins?

North Korea. An entire nation held as a hereditary prison colony for three generations. Political prison camps. Public executions. Engineered famine. The 2014 UN Commission of Inquiry found systematic crimes against humanity revealing "a State that does not have any parallel in the contemporary world." And the world's response is roughly: a shrug and a documentary every few years.

Sudan. Here's the fact I find hardest to explain away, and it's the reason I can't accept the easy answers. From 2004 to 2008, the Save Darfur movement and its student wing put chapters on more than 300 American campuses and drove divestment at 60-plus universities and 27 states. It was backed by the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum and by Elie Wiesel. A movement founded and heavily backed by Jewish institutions, mobilizing American students on behalf of African Muslims.

So the machinery exists. Students can be moved by a distant African genocide. They did it, and it worked.

Today Sudan is worse — 12 million displaced, confirmed famine, El Fasher fallen — and the campuses are quiet. Not quieter. Quiet.

Whatever explains that, it isn't that Sudan is too far away, too complicated, or too African to organize around. We proved otherwise twenty years ago.

The filter nobody sees

You could read everything above and conclude this is a story about human attention — that people are moved by images and narratives, and some crises photograph better than others. That's true, and it isn't the whole of it. The same skew shows up in the institutions we built specifically to correct for human bias.

Take international law. The International Criminal Court has an outstanding warrant against Israel's prime minister. It also has warrants against Vladimir Putin, the Taliban's supreme leader, and Sudan's Omar al-Bashir — plus a prosecutor's pending request for one against Myanmar's junta chief. Al-Bashir was the first sitting head of state ever charged with genocide.

Now consider who can never appear on that list.

The ICC reaches only member states, crimes committed on member-state soil, or cases the UN Security Council refers to it. Iran is not a member. North Korea is not a member. China is not a member — and holds a Security Council veto, as does Russia. So a regime that killed thousands of its own citizens in a single January week cannot be charged. And the 2014 UN finding that North Korea does "not have any parallel in the contemporary world" went precisely nowhere, for exactly the same reason.

This is worth sitting with, because "we uphold international law" sounds like a neutral commitment and operates as a filter. The warrant list is not a ranking of the world's worst actors. It is a ranking of the world's worst actors who happened to be legally reachable — and it is read, almost universally, as the former.

I'm not arguing the court is corrupt. I'm arguing something more troubling: a system can be procedurally honest at every step and still generate an output that misleads everyone who reads it. The gap between "worst" and "reachable" is where a great deal of the world's moral confidence currently lives.

The propaganda machine

I'd have written all of the above a year ago. What has pushed me to actually publish it is watching how quickly the accusations have stopped needing to make sense.

This past week, tens of thousands of people crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta — Spanish authorities put it near 60,000, though every figure in this story is a government estimate of a chaotic event and should be treated as provisional. It is a dispute between Rabat and Madrid over colonial enclaves that predates the modern State of Israel by decades.

Within hours, Israel was blamed.

Not by anonymous accounts. By a spokesman for a Catalan party in the Spanish parliament, who wrote that "the Moroccan dictatorship, in the service of the US and Israel," was using its own people as cannon fodder. By an Oscar-winning actor, to millions of followers. By an American broadcaster on the opposite end of the political spectrum, who announced that Israel had "launched an Islamic invasion of Spain."

Consider what had to be true. Israel would need jurisdiction over Moroccan police, influence over Spanish border enforcement, and a stake in a colonial dispute it has nothing to do with. The far left and the far right arrived at the identical accusation, from opposite directions, inside 48 hours. They didn't land on Morocco, whose citizens crossed. They didn't land on Spanish immigration policy, which Spaniards were themselves loudly debating. They landed on the Jews.

Note what the accusation did not require: evidence, a mechanism, or even internal consistency between the two camps making it. It required only a familiar shape.

This is not new. Within four days of 9/11 — four days — a Syrian state newspaper reported that four thousand Jews had been warned to stay home from the World Trade Center. The number came from a Jerusalem Post article about 4,000 Israelis feared missing in the attacks. It was inverted into an accusation and broadcast by Hezbollah's Al-Manar, and the U.S. State Department was still formally debunking it years later. Ask around. A remarkable number of otherwise reasonable people still half-believe it.

Why it works: the Morgan & Morgan problem

Here's where my day job turns out to be relevant.

I sell real estate. I've spent thirteen years watching what marketing does to belief, and the honest lesson of that career is not flattering to any of us.

If you own a television in this country, you know Morgan & Morgan bills itself as the biggest personal injury firm in America. You know it. You could tell a friend. And their ads have been happy to remind you that size matters.

Ask yourself how you know. You didn't audit their caseload. You saw the billboard on I-95, the bus bench, the commercial in the third quarter, the commercial in the fourth quarter. You saw it four hundred times, and somewhere around the two hundredth it stopped feeling like a claim and started feeling like a fact.

Psychologists have a name for this — the illusory truth effect. Repetition raises the felt truthfulness of a statement, and it works even when you already know better. The reason marketers spend billions on frequency is that frequency isn't a way of communicating truth. It's a substitute for it.

Now apply that to a decade of coverage in which one conflict runs 58 articles a day and the Democratic Republic of the Congo runs 1.5.

I'm not claiming a conspiracy. I don't think there's a room. I think there's something more mundane and much harder to fix: attention compounds. A crisis that's already covered generates student questions, which generate lesson plans, which generate board fights, which generate news coverage of the board fights, which raises salience, which generates more coverage. Sudan never enters the loop, so Sudan never enters the loop.

The story becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. And the sheer volume starts doing the work that evidence is supposed to do.

Where I have to be honest

I promised I'd include what cut against me. Here it is.

The strongest argument on the other side is money, and it's a real argument. The United States has provided Israel roughly $3.8 billion a year under the current memorandum of understanding, plus an $8.7 billion supplemental in April 2024 — at least $16.3 billion in direct military aid since October 7, 2023, by the Council on Foreign Relations' count. American taxpayers are implicated in Gaza in a way they simply are not in Congo. Students who feel a direct line from their taxes to a bomb are not being irrational. They're being citizens.

I don't think it's a complete answer — the UAE, a close American partner, is widely accused of arming the RSF in Sudan, and the U.S. backed the Saudi campaign in Yemen, and neither has generated a single encampment. But it's the best answer the other side has, and I'd be dishonest to wave it off.

There are other real factors too. Gaza is small, dense, and photographed; Sudan and Xinjiang are dark. Palestinian and Jewish communities in America are large and organized, so schools face real local obligations from real families. Seventy-five years of conflict have built up an academic infrastructure that Congo simply doesn't have. Civil wars with six shifting factions are genuinely harder to teach in fifty minutes than a two-sided story.

And the most important caveat of all: the answer to neglect of Sudan is not less attention to Gaza. If this essay gets used to argue that Palestinian children matter less, it has been misread, and I'd rather it not be published than published for that. Nobody has been made safer by treating compassion as a fixed quantity to be rationed.

What I'm arguing for is a consistent standard, not a redistributed silence.

I don't have a fix

I want to end honestly, which means admitting I don't have a solution.

I don't know how you make a newsroom cover Congo. I don't know how you make a student government care about a famine with no footage. I don't know how you get a school board that just spent four hours on a ceasefire resolution to spend twenty minutes on North Korea. I've read both research reports twice, and neither one has an answer either. The best they offer is a method: ask the same questions of every crisis, apply the same rules of evidence, and be honest with students about why one conflict entered the classroom and another never did.

Maybe that's all there is. Not a fix — a discipline.

If we want to be a society that values balanced and accurate information, the work starts with the least comfortable step available to any of us: setting aside our own biases long enough to notice which stories we've been handed, and which ones we were never offered at all.

I've told you mine. That's the only part of this I can actually control.
