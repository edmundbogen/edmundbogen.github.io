The Cloud Needs a Home: What Data Centers Could Mean for South Florida Property Values

Florida spent 2026 deciding how to host the AI boom. The rules it wrote look like good news for homeowners.

The headline you may have seen

On July 15, Palm Beach County commissioners voted 5–1 to turn down Project Tango, a proposed AI data center campus on a 202-acre site in the western county, after a hearing that ran more than twelve hours, according to the county's project record and Commissioner Marci Woodward's published account. On September 24 the commission followed with a unanimous pause of up to one year on new data centers with an anticipated peak demand of 50 megawatts or more in the unincorporated county, as WFLX and CBS12 reported. Smaller facilities can still apply.

The denial was "without prejudice," which means the developer can come back with a revised plan, and earlier approvals on that site remain in place. So this story has more chapters. But I read the last ninety days as a county doing what a careful buyer does: slow down, get the facts, then decide. That is a healthy way to meet a very large opportunity.

Florida wrote the rules early

The bigger news got less attention. Senate Bill 484, signed May 7 and effective July 1, directs the Public Service Commission to keep the costs and non-payment risks of very large power users from landing on everyday customers. It also preserves each local government's authority over land use, adds water-permitting provisions, and required every investor-owned utility to file a large-load tariff by October 1, per the Florida Senate's bill summary.

Florida Power & Light filed on October 1, saying the framework regulators approved in January already does the job. That framework applies to new loads of 50 megawatts or more and includes a 20-year minimum term, a 70 percent minimum take-or-pay obligation and exit fees, according to the PSC's order. The commission still has to review FPL's filing, and that review is the one item on this list I would keep watching.

"The question was never whether the AI economy would want to be in Florida. It was who pays for the wiring. Florida answered that early."

Where the building is actually happening

The nearest real construction pipeline is in Miami-Dade, where Iron Mountain and Metrobloks each describe facilities of under 25 megawatts in their own published materials, well below the scale of the western-county proposal. Broward's footprint is conventional colocation space. On the Treasure Coast the picture is unsettled: a 1,218-acre St. Lucie County land-use application was withdrawn in February, per Data Center Watch, and the developer of a proposed 174-acre site in Fort Pierce tabled its rezoning request on September 17, Treasure Coast Newspapers reported.

Here in Boca Raton, Equinix lists one long-standing facility of roughly 19,000 square feet. Neither research pass I ran for this article turned up a large-scale proposal in or next to Boca Raton or any of the club communities we track.

What the research says about home values

This is where it gets encouraging. The National Association of Realtors published a data center impact report on September 9. According to NAR, the median home value in counties with ten or more data centers is $431,750, against $174,500 in counties with none, and ten-year price growth ran 95 percent against 64 percent. A George Mason University study of 2023 sales in Northern Virginia found that homes nearer data centers tended to sell for more.

The honest caveats matter just as much. NAR's own headline is that "there is no single data center effect," and those prosperous counties were often prosperous first. A 2026 working paper by Michael Rubinovitz found that Loudoun County houses within half a mile of an announced data center sold for roughly 2.8 percent less than similar homes slightly farther away. And NAR found residential electricity rates rose 21.4 percent in high-data-center counties from 2020 to 2024 against 15.7 percent elsewhere, as reported by Scotsman Guide.

Put those together and a clear picture forms. Across a region, the investment, infrastructure and tax base that travel with data centers have gone hand in hand with stronger values. Right next door, a home can take a small discount. And the one cost that reaches everybody, the power bill, is the cost Florida's new law is written to keep where it belongs.

"The communities that come out ahead are the ones that set the terms. Palm Beach County just showed it intends to."

What this means at St. Andrews

For St. Andrews Country Club and communities like it, I see three takeaways. First, nothing large is proposed nearby, and the county's pause makes a surprise application in the unincorporated county unlikely for now. Second, the values here are driven by the things they have always been driven by: the club, the golf and water views, the condition of the home and how few of them come up for sale. Third, the item to watch is your electric bill, and the state has put rules in place around it, pending the PSC's review.

Inside the gates this week there are ten active listings from $2.5 million to $19.975 million, against 26 sales so far this year. In my opinion, a region that draws this much interest from the AI economy, and still keeps control over where it lands, is a region with good options ahead of it. No one can guarantee where prices go from here, and this is market commentary, not investment advice.

The cloud has to live somewhere. South Florida is making sure it arrives on our terms.
