Class Warfare or Fiscal Gamble? What New York's New "Pied-à-Terre Tax" Really Means — and Why the Sixth Borough Is Watching

By Edmund Bogen · Douglas Elliman Real Estate · July 29, 2026
This article was written with the assistance of artificial intelligence.

New York just did something it has debated — and rejected — for more than a decade. On May 28, 2026, Governor Hochul signed the state's first pied-à-terre tax into law, and on July 1 it took effect. If you own a high-value New York City home that isn't your primary residence, you now owe an annual surcharge on top of your regular property tax. Not a one-time transfer tax at closing. Every year.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani didn't soften the message. "On Tax Day earlier this year, I promised that we would tax the rich," he said in July, "and with our new pied-à-terre tax, that is exactly what we have done." His office described the targets as "ultrawealthy out-of-city residents and global elites." Whatever you think of the policy, the framing is unmistakable — and it's the mayor's own. Critics call it class warfare. Supporters call it fairness. As someone who has spent three decades in luxury real estate, I'll tell you what I see in the data: a high-stakes fiscal experiment being run on the most mobile tax base in America.

What the Tax Actually Does

The mechanics matter, because the headline — "a tax on $5 million second homes" — undersells the reach. Through mid-2028, the surcharge applies to one-to-three-family homes valued at $5 million and up at rates of 0.8% to 1.3%. But for condos and co-ops, it starts at just $1 million of the city's assessed market value, at rates of 4% to 6.5%. In 2028, everything moves to a uniform $5 million threshold. The tax is exempt if the home is the owner's primary residence, a close family member's, or is rented to a full-time New York tenant on a lease of at least a year.

The city projects roughly $500 million a year. Its own Comptroller projects less — $340 to $380 million — after accounting for units already rented, valuation quirks, and the most predictable variable in tax policy: people change their behavior. First bills arrive with the January 1, 2027 property-tax cycle, so as of today, not one dollar has actually been collected. Everything is projection.

"The property tax should be levied based on the characteristics of the property, not its ownership." — Citizens Budget Commission

When Has This Ever Worked?

That's the question I keep coming back to, because we don't have to guess. Other governments have run this experiment, and the results are on the record.

France ran the most famous version. Its wealth tax on large fortunes lasted thirty-five years and collected about 63 billion euros over its lifetime. The price, according to French economist Eric Pichet's analysis: roughly 200 billion euros in capital flight, tens of thousands of millionaire households gone, and lost income-tax, sales-tax, and growth revenue that likely cost the French state about twice what the tax ever raised. President Macron — no one's idea of a radical — repealed it in 2017.

Norway ran the fast-motion version. In 2022 its government raised the wealth tax expecting roughly $146 million a year in new revenue. Within two years, some 300 of the country's wealthiest taxpayers — including one of its richest men — had relocated to Switzerland, and analysts tallied the annual revenue lost at roughly four times the projected gain. I'll be fair: other researchers argue the Norwegian exodus is overstated. But no one argues it produced the money it promised.

The United Kingdom is running the live version right now. After abolishing its centuries-old "non-dom" regime in April 2025, Britain is projected to lose a record sixteen thousand five hundred millionaires in a single year — more than any country in the world — and London just slipped from fifth to sixth among the world's wealthiest cities.

And we've seen the American micro-version, one taxpayer at a time. When hedge-fund founder David Tepper moved from New Jersey to Florida in 2016, the state's own budget analysts warned his departure alone could open a hole of up to $140 million a year. Connecticut lost Paul Tudor Jones and Edward Lampert to Florida and reached the point where its revenue department was personally monitoring its hundred largest taxpayers — because just 357 families were paying fourteen percent of the state's entire income tax.

Honesty requires the counterexample too: Maryland's 2008 millionaire surcharge is often cited as proof of flight — millionaire filers dropped thirty percent — but later analysis showed most of that was the recession shrinking incomes, not moving vans. The research consensus is actually narrower and more sobering than either side admits: average millionaires mostly stay put. The ultra-mobile top — billionaires, founders, fund principals — move. And in New York City, where taxpayers earning over $10 million are 0.04% of filers yet pay nearly eighteen percent of the income tax, the ultra-mobile top is the budget.

Why the Math Should Worry New York

Here's what the concentration looks like in full. Filers earning over $1 million are just 0.9% of the city's full-year resident taxpayers — and they pay 37.6% of its personal income tax. The securities industry alone generated $6.7 billion in city revenue last fiscal year. Real estate transaction taxes added $2 billion more. When your revenue is that concentrated, you don't need an exodus to have a problem. You need only attrition at the margin.

New York has run this experiment on itself before. In the 1970s, the federal government's own auditors concluded that the erosion of the city's economic base — people, jobs, and businesses leaving — was "at the root" of the fiscal crisis that nearly bankrupted it. IRS data show New York lost roughly $77 billion in net adjusted gross income to other states between 2019 and 2023, second only to California. Nobody can attribute those moves to any single tax; migration data show correlation, not causation. But when the city's stated strategy is to lean harder on the very households doing the moving, the direction of risk is not a mystery.

To be fair to the other side of the ledger: the flight hasn't shown up yet. Manhattan luxury contract activity held steady through spring, and the leading market trackers say there is no measurable "Mamdani effect" so far. And the strongest argument for the tax is real — New York's property-tax system genuinely is broken. State law forces the city to value luxury condos as if they were rentals, which is how a $238 million penthouse can carry a city valuation somewhere between $9 million and $15 million. Even the tax's fiercest critics concede that inequity. The second half of 2026 — the first bills, the first full selling season under the new regime — will be the real test.

The Sixth Borough

Now let me be clear about where I stand, because it's not where you might expect.

Yes — Florida, Texas, and other low- and no-tax states reap the benefit every time a high-tax state declares war on its own tax base. More than $1 trillion in investment assets moved to Florida between 2020 and 2023. South Florida now hosts thousands of financial firms employing a quarter-million people — they call it Wall Street South for a reason. West Palm Beach and Miami rank among the fastest-growing wealth hubs on earth.

But here's what I tell every New Yorker who calls me: we don't need to succeed this way. Florida's case doesn't depend on New York failing, and I take no pleasure in watching the city that built so many of my clients' fortunes — and so many of their family stories — talk about them like adversaries.

Because our connection to New York isn't competitive. It's familial. Boca Raton has been the Sixth Borough for half a century — since Century Village and Kings Point were marketed straight to middle-class New Yorkers looking for sunshine. The grandparents came for the winters. Their children came for the schools and the space. Now the firms have followed the families. When New Yorkers move into our country club communities from Boca Raton to Delray Beach, they aren't fleeing anything — they're coming home to a place their own families helped build. That's not conquest. That's continuity.

Our connection to New York isn't competitive. It's familial. Boca Raton has been the Sixth Borough for half a century.

So my message cuts both ways. To New York: your greatest export has never been taxes collected — it's been talented, ambitious people, and you should fight to keep them, not lecture them. To my readers: if you're re-running the math on where your next decade should be based, you don't have to wait for a verdict on this experiment. The Sixth Borough has always kept a light on for you.

One more thing worth knowing: the city has made its enforcement roll a public record. The Department of Finance published a supplemental valuation roll in late July listing properties potentially subject to the surcharge — a database that swept in far more than the "global elites" of the press releases, and promptly ignited a privacy backlash. You can check any property — including your own building — on the Department of Finance's official surcharge page at nyc.gov/npsurcharge. I'd encourage you to look not for names, but for what the list represents: a government publishing its residents' holdings in a way critics see as leverage. That, more than any rate schedule, tells you where this is headed.

The Bottom Line

The pied-à-terre tax will raise real money — somewhere between the Comptroller's caution and the mayor's confidence. It will push some empty apartments into use. It will not fix New York's affordability crisis, because the evidence from every city that has tried this says it won't. What it will do is test, in real time, whether a city can build its budget on a narrow class of taxpayers while publicly campaigning against them. France ran that test for thirty-five years and repealed it. Norway ran it and, by the leading estimates, lost roughly four dollars for every one it hoped to gain. New York has the history — its own history — to know better. And the Sixth Borough, as always, will keep the light on.
