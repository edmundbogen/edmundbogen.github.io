The Club Is the New Address: Why Luxury Buyers Are Choosing Boca Raton's Country Club Communities in 2026

Palm Beach County leads America for net wealth migration — and an outsized share of those buyers are landing behind gates.

The wealth is still arriving — and it is choosing gates

The migration headline of 2026 belongs to Palm Beach County. According to IRS interstate-migration data reported in July, the county gained roughly $3 billion in net adjusted gross income from inbound tax filers — the largest gain of any county in the United States, ahead of Collier County at about $2.25 billion, while Los Angeles County and Manhattan posted multibillion- and near-billion-dollar losses. Florida's zero personal income tax, confirmed by the Florida Department of Revenue, remains the structural draw for high earners establishing residency.

Honesty requires the other half of the picture. U.S. Census Bureau estimates show Florida's net domestic migration cooled to 22,517 in the year ended June 30, 2025, down from 310,892 in 2022, even as the state led the nation in international migration at 178,674. The firehose has become a filter: fewer movers overall, but the ones arriving skew wealthier — and disproportionately, they are buying behind gates.

"Buyers are not purchasing a house that happens to sit beside a golf course. They are buying a managed ecosystem with a ready-made community attached."

Club economics are the demand signal

Nothing says demand like repricing. Broker-reported fee schedules compiled this year show St. Andrews Country Club's membership contribution rising from $450,000 to $500,000 effective September 1, 2026 — roughly $550,000 all-in with the property owners' association contribution — while Boca West moves to a $215,000 joining fee on October 1, Woodfield to $300,000 on November 2, and Addison Reserve reached $400,000 on January 1. When every club on the corridor raises its entry fee in the same year, that is not coincidence — it is a demand signal.

The clubs are earning those numbers with capital. St. Andrews completed a $27.5 million enhancement program — a redesigned clubhouse, rebuilt lakeside dining, a new pool pavilion and a 19,000-square-foot fitness center. Broken Sound's own media materials report a $40 million clubhouse renovation, and Boca West broke ground in April on a complete redesign of its Golf Course 1. The product being sold is no longer golf; it is wellness, racquet sports, children's programming and a social calendar — the amenities a visibly younger membership is paying for. National industry data points the same direction: club waitlists are up 24 percent since 2019 and median initiation fees have climbed steeply, with buyers in their 30s and early 40s now a major growth demographic.

The condo reset is redirecting buyers

A 2026-specific tailwind deserves plain naming. As of January 1, 2026, Florida law — Section 718.112 of the Florida Statutes, passed after the Surfside tragedy — requires condo buildings of three stories or more to complete milestone structural inspections and fully fund reserves. The resulting special assessments and rising monthly budgets have pushed a meaningful share of affluent downsizers and seasonal buyers out of the coastal condo market and toward fee-simple homes in gated club communities, where the carrying costs are transparent and the building is your own. Florida Realtors data shows the cash-heavy character of this buyer pool: 26.9 percent of statewide single-family closings in August 2026 were all-cash — buyers largely insulated from mortgage rates.

What the ceiling looks like

The top of the Boca market reset in May, when a Royal Palm Yacht & Country Club spec estate at 2500 E. Maya Palm Drive closed at $75 million — a city record and, per Redfin, the most expensive U.S. home sale of the month. Records at the ceiling do not reprice every home below them, but they recalibrate what global buyers believe Boca Raton is.

What this means at St. Andrews

Inside the gates, our own BeachesMLS pull as of October 6 shows eight active single-family listings in St. Andrews Country Club, priced from $2.5 million to $19.975 million, with 22 homes sold year-to-date. That is a tight, selective market — balanced, not frenzied. Homes that make the club lifestyle immediately usable — new construction and turnkey renovations — are commanding disproportionate attention, because a buyer writing a six-figure membership check at closing has little appetite for a second construction project. In my opinion, the fee ladder now being set across the corridor effectively raises the floor under resale values in mandatory-membership communities; a locked-in membership is becoming a six-figure asset attached to the house. Pricing discipline still matters — this is a negotiable market, and sellers who respect that are the ones closing.
