The Club Is the New Address: Why Luxury Buyers Are Choosing Boca Raton's Country Club Communities in 2026

Money has been moving to Palm Beach County for five straight years. What changed in 2026 is where it parks.

Follow the money

Palm Beach County ranks first among all U.S. counties for net incoming income, with roughly $23 billion in net domestic income gained from 2019 through 2023, according to a Miami Realtors analysis of IRS migration data. And the buyers arriving are not stretching: through August, South Florida million-dollar sales were up 21.7 percent year over year — nearly three times the growth of the overall market — with 60 percent of those purchases paid in cash, per Miami Realtors. Palm Beach County alone accounted for 30 percent of the region's million-dollar transactions.

At the very top, the county broke its full-year record for $10 million-plus home sales in September — with more than three months of 2026 still on the clock, per Miami Realtors.

"The wealth is here. The question buyers are really asking is which address gives them certainty."

The condo reset is a referendum on predictability

Florida's post-Surfside reserve law is now fully in force. Condo associations in buildings three stories and up had to complete structural-integrity reserve studies by December 31, 2025, and owners generally can no longer vote to waive the reserves that fund roofs, structure, waterproofing and other big-ticket components (Section 718.112, Florida Statutes). The result is honest pricing of deferred maintenance — and, in older buildings, special assessments that arrive as surprises.

To be fair, this is not a countywide condo crash. Palm Beach County's condo median actually rose 5.26 percent year over year in August to $300,000, per Miami Realtors — the pain is building-by-building, concentrated in older towers. But the supply math tells you where confidence sits: 3.5 months of single-family inventory versus 6.7 months on the condo side. Buyers with options are choosing the asset class where the costs are known in advance.

That is exactly the trade a club community offers. The check is bigger up front, but it is disclosed, predictable and attached to equity and lifestyle — not to an engineering report you haven't seen yet.

The price of admission keeps rising

Here is the pattern I find most telling. Within roughly thirteen months, nearly every major Boca Raton club raised its cost of entry, according to published 2026 membership guides: Broken Sound made its contributions non-refundable as of October 2025; Boca West's initiation is reported to rise from $150,000 to $215,000 effective October 1, 2026; Woodfield's full equity from $225,000 to $300,000 effective November 2, 2026; and St. Andrews Country Club's joining contribution moved to $500,000 — $550,000 all-in with the property owners association — effective September 1, 2026. Clubs do not publish fee schedules publicly, so verify current figures with the club directly before contracting.

"Boards do not vote for increases like these into weak demand."

The clubs are also spending like they intend to keep winning: Broken Sound cites a $40 million clubhouse renovation and a $7 million spa, Woodfield a $7.9 million golf course renovation, and St. Andrews completed a $27.5 million enhancement program. A GGA Partners/CMAA study of club leaders found cost concerns concentrated among older members — 58 percent versus 17 percent for younger ones — while younger members engage most with wellness, racquets and social programming. The renovation arms race is aimed squarely at the next generation of members, and in Boca the luxury buyer now averages about 45 years old, per Behind the Hedges.

What this means at St. Andrews

Inside St. Andrews Country Club, the market this week is eight active listings from $2.5 million to $19.975 million — including brand-new 2026–2027 construction — against 26 sales so far this year. That is a community turning over roughly three to four percent of its homes annually while its board raises the cost of entry. In my opinion, that combination — thin supply, rising admission, heavy reinvestment — is what a market looks like before it reprices upward, though no one can guarantee where prices go from here.

One honest caveat: club ownership carries its own risks. It swaps the condo world's surprise assessments for large disclosed contributions, annual dues and the possibility of future increases. The buyers winning in 2026 are not fleeing one cost for another — they are paying for certainty, and treating the club itself as part of the asset.

The address used to be the house. In 2026, it's the club.
