The Age Premium

Half the homes for sale inside the gates were built — or will be built — in the last seven years. The gap between new and vintage pricing is now the defining feature of this market.
By Edmund Bogen · August 19, 2026 · Written with the assistance of artificial intelligence — all figures drawn from BeachesMLS as of August 19, 2026

Four days ago, a brand-new listing hit the market inside St. Andrews Country Club: 17903 Foxborough Lane, a just-completed 2026 build with 7,616 square feet under air, asking $10,150,000. That works out to roughly $1,333 per square foot of living area.

Three streets away, another Foxborough Lane home — 3,337 square feet, built in 1984 — is asking $3,995,000, or about $1,197 per square foot. And a 7,709-square-foot lakefront home on Scarsdale Way, built in 2005, is asking $5,850,000. That's $759 per square foot — not much more than half the rate of the new build up the street.

One community. One gate. Eight active listings. And the single biggest factor separating a $759-per-foot ask from a $2,566-per-foot ask isn't lot, lake, or golf view. It's the year on the certificate of occupancy.

The pattern is hard to miss. The four homes built before 2010 are asking roughly $759 to $1,197 per square foot. The three completed homes built 2019 or later are asking roughly $1,317 to $2,566. Even setting aside the $19.975M lakefront showpiece — which carries a waterfront premium doing some of that work — the newest inventory starts its pricing about where the vintage inventory tops out.

"The newest inventory starts its pricing about where the vintage inventory tops out."

Why the gap exists

Part of this is simple replacement cost. Land inside an established club is finite, construction costs have risen sharply over the past decade, and a buyer who wants new either builds it — years of carrying costs, approvals, and decisions — or pays someone who already did. The spec builder's premium is, in part, a convenience fee on time.

Part of it is buyer preference. In my experience inside these gates, a meaningful share of today's luxury buyers — especially those relocating from the Northeast — want turn-key. They will pay decisively for a home where the only decision left is the furniture, and they will discount aggressively for a home that needs a renovation they don't want to manage from a thousand miles away.

And notice what the market is telling us about velocity: the 2026 build listed four days ago. The 1987 home has been on the market 145 days. Freshness sells in both senses of the word.

If you own a vintage home here

This is not bad news — it's a pricing framework. Your competition is not "the house down the street." It's the per-square-foot ladder above. A well-renovated older home can close a large part of the age gap; an original-condition home cannot, no matter how it's marketed. The honest question at listing time is which rung your home's condition actually occupies — and pricing to that rung is what produces a sale instead of a 145-day listing.

If you're buying

The vintage discount is the opportunity. A structurally excellent 2005 home at $759 per foot, in the same community where new construction asks $1,333, leaves real room to renovate to your own taste and still be in at a sensible basis. The buyers who do best in this market are the ones who can see past a dated kitchen to the spread in the table above.

The wider context

St. Andrews currently shows 8 active listings against 19 sales year-to-date — roughly 3.2 months of inventory, which still favors sellers by any conventional standard. Across the 21 luxury club communities we track from Boca Raton to Delray Beach, there are 193 active listings and 635 sales year-to-date. The full community-by-community table is on this week's dashboard.

One number to leave you with: of the eight homes you can buy inside St. Andrews today, four were built — or will be built — since 2019. In a 40-year-old community, half the shelf is new. That, more than any single price, tells you where this market is going.
