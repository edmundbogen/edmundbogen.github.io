A $3,899 bill that ended at $65

Earlier this year I had an outpatient hip replacement. Months later, a bill arrived from the hospital demanding $3,899.42.

Something about it didn't sit right with me, so I sat down with an AI assistant and started asking questions. It helped me request the right paperwork, line up the insurer's statement against the hospital's bill, and spot the problem: the implant had been processed as durable medical equipment, at 50 percent coinsurance, instead of under my outpatient surgery benefit.

My appeal was denied with boilerplate language. So, again with AI's help, I escalated, including a complaint to the Florida Department of Financial Services. A few weeks after that complaint, the insurer agreed the implant had been processed incorrectly and reprocessed the claim.

My remaining balance came to $65. No lawyer, no billing advocate, and it cost me nothing but my time — about five and a half months from my first dispute letter to the reprocessed claim.

I'm not telling you this to brag. I'm telling you because a year or two ago, I wouldn't have known where to start. I would have paid the bill.

The impossible became likely

What struck me most wasn't the money. It was how ordinary the whole thing felt.

Reading an explanation of benefits used to be a job for a specialist. Drafting a formal appeal used to mean hiring someone. Knowing which state office to call used to take a friend who happened to know. None of that is true anymore. Things we assumed were impossible for regular people are now likely, and in many cases probable.

That shift is happening in the most mundane corners of life, and it's happening fast.

My mother and the MP3 player

Years ago, my mother told me she wasn't going to buy an MP3 player unless they promised it was the last thing they were ever going to invent.

We laughed. But I think about that line all the time now, because it has never been more relevant. Nobody is going to make that promise. The tools are going to keep changing, faster than any of us would like. Waiting for things to settle down means waiting forever.

How I use it every day

As a realtor, I use AI in nearly everything I do: generating market reports, doing detailed research on properties and neighborhoods, and helping create images like the one at the top of this email. It has made me faster and, honestly, better at my job.

But I want to be clear about one thing. This is not about replacing the professionals in your life. I still rely on my doctors, my accountant, my attorney and my insurance broker. What AI changes is how prepared I am when I walk into the room with them. I find that incredibly empowering, and I'd love for every one of you to feel it too.

One thing you can try this week

Here's a simple example. The next time you get results from a medical exam or blood test, ask an AI assistant to explain them to you in plain language, and then ask it: "If you were my doctor, what questions should I be asking you about these results?"

Bring that list to your appointment. You'll walk in understanding your own results and walk out with better answers. It's available to you right now, and it's a game changer.

A few sensible habits: take out your name, date of birth and account numbers before you paste anything in, and treat what you get back as a starting point for the conversation with your doctor, not a substitute for it. AI makes mistakes. In my own billing fight it got a calculation wrong early on, and I only caught it because I checked its work against the original documents.

Where else this helps right now

Medical bills are just the opening act. The same half hour of asking questions works on the renewal letter from your insurance carrier, the 60-page inspection report on a home you're buying, a contractor's estimate, an HOA notice, a lease, a closing statement, a letter from the IRS. Paste it in, ask what it says in plain language, ask what's missing, ask what you should push back on. You don't have to accept the first version of anything anymore.

And if you run a business, as many of you do, I'll be more direct: the threat of business loss is real. Your clients are already walking in with AI-prepared questions, and your competitors are already using these tools to answer faster, follow up better and do more with the same day. I see it inside my own industry every week. At the same time — what really is the alternative? I ask that in all sincerity. Sitting it out doesn't make the change slower; it just decides which side of it you're on.

Interview your professionals — including me

Here's a use most people haven't thought of yet: before you hire a professional, let AI help you run the interview. Choosing a realtor, a financial advisor, a contractor, an estate attorney — these are decisions most of us make a handful of times in our lives, against someone who has the conversation every week. AI levels that.

Try this before your next listing appointment or buyer consultation — with me or with anyone else. Tell an AI assistant: "I'm interviewing a real estate agent to sell my home in a country club community in Boca Raton. Give me ten questions that would reveal whether they actually know this market, how they'll price and market my home, what their communication looks like after the agreement is signed, and what in their track record I should verify. Then tell me what a weak answer to each question sounds like." That last part is the secret — knowing what a weak answer sounds like is what interviewers get paid for.

Then do the same with the paperwork: paste in the listing agreement or buyer agreement before you sign and ask what each clause means, what's negotiable, and what questions to ask about it. A good professional will welcome every bit of this. I mean it when I say it about myself: a client who shows up with AI-prepared questions makes the appointment better, because we skip the brochure talk and get straight to the decisions that matter.

A little theory: what these tools do to the way we think

One more thing worth knowing, because it isn't all upside. Researchers at the MIT Media Lab spent four months watching people write essays while wearing EEG headsets — one group used ChatGPT, one used a search engine, one used nothing but their own head. The brain-only group showed the strongest and most widespread neural connectivity; the AI group showed the weakest, and minutes after finishing, most of the AI users couldn't quote a line from their own essay. The researchers call it "cognitive debt": let the tool do the thinking, and your brain politely declines to engage.

Psychologists have a name for the broader pattern: cognitive offloading — the same reason none of us know phone numbers anymore. The lesson isn't to avoid the tools. It's the order of operations. The MIT group that did their own thinking first and then brought in AI showed the best of both worlds. That's exactly how I used it on my hospital bill: I read the documents myself, formed my own view, then put AI to work. Use it as a sparring partner, not a chauffeur.

A moment of reflection

So here is what I'd ask of you. Take a minute this week and ask yourself honestly: am I keeping up with these tools, or am I waiting for someone to promise it's the last thing they'll invent?

I don't want anyone reading this to be left behind on it. We've all adapted to every big change that came our way, and this one is no different. The only real mistake is deciding it isn't for us.
